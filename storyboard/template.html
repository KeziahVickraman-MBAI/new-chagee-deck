Two kinds of technology meet there. Predictive AI forecasts demand: where to open, and how busy the hour will be. Physical AI, automation and machines, does the fixed steps. Around them, people share the hand steps like a bucket brigade.

Our decision-maker throughout is the GM, Chagee Singapore's general manager. Timing: title and agenda together, about twenty seconds.

This answers part c of the brief: how big is the benefit, for Singapore only. Against what: a flat normal-day roster and an all-by-hand counter. We do not know how Chagee rosters today, so that is a question for Chagee.

Lead with orders. On an event day the model keeps about {{r.ben_event_orders}} orders per outlet in the peak hour that would otherwise walk away, about S${{r.ben_event_rev}} of revenue; on a busy day about {{r.ben_busy_orders}}. Over a year it depends on how many such days there are: our illustrative calendar is {{sb.busy_days_per_year}} busy days, the weekends, and {{sb.event_days_per_year}} event days, the public holidays. That is an input, not a measurement.

Profit is the second line, using a margin we assume because Chagee does not disclose one. The labour saving is shown separately and never added to revenue. The two levers are run together, not added, because they share the same labour hours. The strip at the top splits the profit: most is counter automation, physical AI, the machine on its own; the forecast, predictive AI, adds a small share on top.

Two crew rules for rostering to the forecast. The walk-away rule adds people until peak-hour walk-aways are within {{sb.walkaway_target|pct}}; at our default volume that is exactly the forecast crew. The economic rule adds a person only when the orders kept are worth more than the extra hours. They differ on busy days by hand, where the economic rule adds no one.

Be honest about fragility: most of the profit is the counter's labour saving, and it comes from the model's minimum crew, {{r.min_hand}} by hand and {{r.min_mixed}} with machines. Drop that rule and the profit falls to about S${{r.ben_profit_fragile}} a year per outlet. And rostering to the forecast pays on event days, not on busy days, where the extra hours cost more than the orders won back. These are modelled avoided costs on stated assumptions, not demonstrated savings. About one minute.

Takeaway: Most modelled profit is counter labour, and it rests on the minimum-crew rule (S${{r.ben_profit}} falls to S${{r.ben_profit_fragile}} a year per outlet without it). The forecast pays on event days; on busy days only the economic rule avoids a loss.

Appendix · How the site forecast works (slide 05)

DBSCAN finds where searches bunch up; a proximity model turns a pin into cups

DBSCAN in four moves · toy example
  1. 1Radius ε. Two searches are neighbours if they are within ε of each other (the cluster-radius slider).
  2. 2Core. A search with at least the minimum number of searches within ε (counting itself) is a core point.
  3. 3Grow. A cluster spreads from core to core. Searches it reaches that are not core join as border points.
  4. 4Noise. Searches no cluster reaches. Unlike k-means, nobody picks the number of clusters, and odd shapes are fine.

Unsupervised: no labels or past openings are needed. Ester, Kriegel, Sander and Xu (1996). On slide 05 it runs only on searches with no outlet within walking distance; each cluster's centre is a candidate site.
