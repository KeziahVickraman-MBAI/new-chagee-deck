How the logic works ▸
  • A supply chain is a chain of buffers. Goods flow from suppliers through warehouses to stores. When demand misses the forecast, the miss is absorbed wherever something can still change in time.
  • Rigid: fixed upstream, so the miss lands in the store. Chagee sources tea and milk directly and distributes from HQ for its domestic stores (L-prn); we read overseas sourcing as standardised too. Origin and routes are set long before opening, so in the first weeks only the store can react: its opening stock and its counter speed.
  • Localised: suppliers nearby can top up. A local supplier can resend stock quickly, so part of a stock miss is absorbed upstream. The counter still has to serve the queue on the day, so it stays exposed.
  • So our slice. We take sourcing as given and work on the two buffers a new store controls: opening stock (Topics 4 and 5, Provision) and counter speed (Topic 5, Counter).

Predict: plan on a demand range, not one number, and treat the range as an assumption to test

GATE 1 · PREDICT
Decision

What demand should the whole store's inventory and counter planning assume?

Suggested action

Plan on about 3,889 cups a week, within an illustrative 80% range of 2,625 to 5,153; treat it as an assumption to test.

Evidence
Base3,889 cups/week80% range2,625–5,153Half-width±32.5%
Trade-off

Plan too low: stockouts, queues and lost sales. Plan too high: stock that expires or ties up cash, and machines or staff that sit idle.

Limitation

The range is assumed, not calibrated, and demand can fall outside it. It cannot approve a site or show that a store is viable.

Who does what Market-entry leadsMembership / CRM
  1. Membership / CRM Before the site is committed, open a pre-launch waitlist and tag each sign-up as new or already a Chagee member in another market.
  2. Market-entry leads Set the base from comparable stores; use sign-ups only to cross-check it. Send the range to supply chain (slide 4) and to store format (slide 10).
  3. Market-entry leads Widen the range where tourists or walk-ups matter, and back-test it on recent openings before narrowing it.
How it is calculated

Base = an assumed comparable-store volume, 200,000 cups a year over 360 open days (synthetic).

Range = base, plus or minus the half-width. The half-width moves in a straight line from ±50% with no signal to ±15% with a strong one: an assumption to test, not evidence that membership data improves accuracy.

On the range alone the counter choice changes only beyond ±57%.

Provision: translate the sales forecast into an opening inventory plan

GATE 2 · PROVISION
Decision

What must be on hand on opening day, what should the first delivery bring, and what is replenished later?

Suggested action

Build the opening list item by item from the demand range: tea, milk and alternatives, other ingredients, cups, lids and packaging. Size fresh milk with the worked example.

Evidence
Quantified herefresh milk only (slide 5)Needs Chagee datamenu mix · recipes · lead times · minimum orders
Trade-off

Too little of any one item stops sales. Too much ties up cash and storage, and perishables expire or lose quality.

Limitation

No quantities for tea, other ingredients or packaging: their inputs are not in our sources. Inventory and counter are not optimised together.

Who does what Supply chain / planningMarket-entry leads
  1. Supply chain / planning Collect recipes, the expected menu mix, and each supplier's lead time and minimum order for the market.
  2. Supply chain / planning Draft the opening list: requirement = forecast drinks × amount per drink, net of stock on hand and deliveries already due.
  3. Market-entry leads Send any change to the demand range, including expected opening hype; supply chain recomputes the list.
  4. Store operations Count stock at opening and log stockouts of every item for Review.
How it is calculated

Ingredient requirement = forecast drink quantities × ingredient amount per drink.

Purchase order = requirement over the cover period − usable stock − deliveries already due + expected losses, rounded to the supplier's minimum and delivery days.

Reorder point: reorder when stock falls to the usage expected during the lead time, plus safety stock for demand above that. Explained here; not implemented in the prototype.

Implemented in the prototype: the first fresh-milk delivery only (slide 5).

From forecast to purchase order

1

The whole store's weekly range (slide 3). Cups alone are not enough: drinks use different ingredients.

2

Product mix

Share of each drink and format (size, hot or cold). Requires menu-mix data.

3

Recipes and materials

Ingredient requirement = forecast drink quantities × ingredient amount per drink. Packaging follows orders and formats.

4

Deliveries and stock

The order nets off usable stock and deliveries due, adds expected losses, and fits lead times, shelf life, storage and supplier minimums.

GATE 2 · PROVISION · FRESH MILK
Decision

How much fresh milk should the first delivery bring?

Suggested action

Steady demand: about 788 L for the first 7 days. If the expected opening surge is real, about 1,182 L: this delivery falls inside it.

Evidence
Short vs waste, per LS$25.47 vs S$2.55Order covers91% of outcomesExpected waste209 LExpected shortage42 cups
Trade-off

Order less: more missed cups at S$3.82 each. Order more: more milk thrown away at S$2.55 a litre. A shortage costs more, so the order sits above base demand.

Limitation

Sizes one delivery of one item; it does not optimise the whole inventory. Still runs short in about 9% of outcomes. Generic milk costs, one amount per cup.

Who does what Supply chain / planningMarket-entry leads
  1. Market-entry leads Say whether the opening surge is expected; size the first delivery on it if so.
  2. Supply chain / planning Place the first delivery at the suggested order, not at the base forecast or the top of the range.
  3. Store operations From the second delivery on, order on actual sales; log waste and shortages for Review.
How it is calculated

Newsvendor logic: order up to the point where the chance of running short equals waste cost ÷ (shortage + waste cost) per litre = S$2.55 ÷ (S$25.47 + S$2.55), so the order covers about 91% of outcomes.

Shortage cost per litre = S$3.82 per missed cup ÷ 0.15 L per cup, the same for every drink (a simplification).

The best order is 9.5 days of the demand it is sized on, just above the top of the 80% range (9.3 days), because a shortage costs far more than waste.

Surge: opening demand is assumed 1.5× steady demand for 2 weeks, longer than this 7-day delivery, so the same calculation on surge demand gives the larger order.

Counter flow: people pass work along like a bucket brigade; machines are fixed, so they queue at the bottleneck or sit idle

Bucket brigade Course concept · Topic 5 Illustration with the Week 5 rules Workers pass each order along a line and rebalance themselves, slowest first. Informs: how much a crew absorbs in a surge

Bottleneck Course concept · Topic 5 Illustration and cost model The slowest step sets the pace of the whole line. Informs: where a fixed machine line queues

People pass the work along

Each worker carries a drink downstream and hands it over. In a surge the handover point moves, so the load evens out: one way a crew shares work.

Machines stay at their station

A fixed station cannot hand work back or take on more. The queue builds at the slowest one, wherever it sits on the line.

Faster in the peak, idle at low volume

Machines can cut the wait in a surge, but sized for the peak they are busy only part of the day, and idle capacity is what a store pays for.

Limitation

Who does what Store operationsMarket-entry leads
  1. Market-entry leads Send store operations the hourly forecast, including the opening-week hype.
  2. Store operations Publish the suggested roster; in opening weeks add the extra shifts only at the peaks.
  3. Store operations Each week, rerun with actual hourly sales and measured waits; change the roster only if the service level or cost moves.
How it is calculated

Per hour: the fewest people (queueing model, one shared line) who serve 80% of customers within 5 minutes and are busy at most 85%, at forecast × 1.2×.

Shifts: the set with the fewest paid hours covering every half-hour, 4 to 8 hours, a break in shifts over 6 hours, at most 8 people. The search is exhaustive, so the result is the lowest cost within these rules.

Baseline: a fixed crew sized for the busiest hour, every open hour.

The operational problem

Demand peaks at lunch and in the evening. A fixed crew sized for the busiest hour pays for quiet hours; a thin crew lets peak queues grow.

How AI contributes

AI forecasts each hour's demand (Predict). An optimiser, not AI, then sets the roster: forecasting and workforce optimisation are separate steps.

Optimisation logic

Each hour: the fewest people serving 80% within 5 minutes at under 85% busy (a queueing model). Then the cheapest 4–8-hour shifts, with breaks, for at most 8 people.

Expected business impact

Steady demand: the minimum crew of 2 covers every hour, so no saving. Opening weeks: 30 paid hours a day against 36 for a fixed crew, about S$1,265 per opening (illustrative).

Limitations

Textbook queue (random arrivals, one shared line), whole-hour shift starts, no absences or skill mix. Illustrative inputs; not Chagee's rostering system.

Appendix A2 · the uncertainty sweep · handout only

At a new store’s volume, a mixed people-and-machine counter beats full automation, because machines sit idle, not because demand is uncertain

We tested the hypothesis that mixed wins only until demand becomes predictable. Our simulator rejected the uncertainty part.

Machines come in whole units, so they sit idle

Even one machine per station serves about 240 cups an hour against a forecast peak of about 61. The fully automated line is busy only 21% to 22% of open hours.

People flex; machines cannot be returned

Rosters follow the hour and the real demand, and staff rebalance across stations like a bucket brigade. A mixed counter buys 3 machines instead of 6, with similar staff hours.

Where machines still win

Big surges: at a 2.5× peak, people-based counters lose about 11% of orders and full automation is cheaper. Also steady high volume, consistency and labour scarcity.
